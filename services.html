The offer

Five ways we help

Five patterns we keep seeing, written to the same structure so they read as one system: the situation in the client's words, what's really going on underneath it, what we do, what they walk away with, and how we'll know it worked. None of these are mutually exclusive — a client often starts in one and surfaces a second.

Pillar 01

AI orientation & opportunity mapping

The situation

"We keep hearing we should be doing something with AI. We don't know where to start, and we don't want to waste six months on a pilot nobody uses."

What's really going on

Leadership has real signal that competitors or vendors are moving, but no way to translate "AI" into "which specific things in our product and delivery process would benefit, in what order, for what return." Without that translation, the default move is a splashy pilot disconnected from the roadmap, or nothing at all.

What we do

  • Interview product, engineering, support and — where relevant — sales, to find where the business already loses time or money to well-understood, repeatable work.
  • Map candidate AI applications against effort, risk, and customer-facing value, not against what's technically impressive.
  • Rank them into a short list with a plain-language case for each: what it costs to try, what it's worth if it works, how you'd know within weeks, not quarters.

What you walk away with

  • A prioritized opportunity map, not a slide of buzzwords.
  • A recommended first move, sized to prove value fast.
  • A shared vocabulary the leadership team can actually use with each other.

How we know it worked

  • You can name the top 1–3 AI opportunities and defend the ranking without us in the room.
  • The first pilot ships, and its outcome is measured against the case we wrote for it.

Pillar 02

From playground to production

The situation

"Half our engineers are already using AI tools on their own. Some of it's great, some of it's a liability, and we have no idea which is which."

What's really going on

Individual initiative outpaced organizational direction. Ungoverned use isn't the real problem — the absence of a bar for what "production quality" means for an AI-assisted feature is. No review standard, no evaluation process, no shared pattern library, so quality is whoever built it that week.

What we do

  • Audit what's actually running today — tools, prompts, integrations, and everywhere model output reaches a customer or a decision.
  • Establish a production bar: review criteria, an evaluation/testing approach for AI-assisted or AI-driven features, guardrails for known failure modes.
  • Train the team against that bar using their own codebase — a pattern library and a review checklist, not a generic course.

What you walk away with

  • A written standard for shipping AI-assisted work, folded into your existing review process.
  • The best of what your team already built, hardened and kept; the rest retired or fixed, deliberately.
  • Engineers who can tell the difference between a demo and a dependency.

How we know it worked

  • AI-assisted features go through the same review rigor as anything else you ship — no special exception, no special fear.
  • Incident and rollback rates for AI-touched features trend to the same baseline as the rest of the codebase.

Pillar 03

AI cost efficiency

The situation

"Our AI bill keeps climbing and nobody can tell me if we're getting more value for it or just more usage."

What's really going on

Token and API spend is easy to turn on and hard to attribute — which team, which feature, which model tier is driving it, and whether a cheaper model or a smarter architecture would do the same job. Without attribution, "cut the budget" and "let it ride" are the only two levers anyone can pull.

What we do

  • Break down current spend by team, feature, and model/provider, and map it against the value each use actually delivers.
  • Identify the easy wins: right-sizing model choice, caching, prompt/context efficiency, batching, and eliminating usage nobody is actually reading.
  • Set up ongoing visibility — a dashboard or a reporting cadence — so cost-per-outcome is a number someone owns, not a surprise on the invoice.

What you walk away with

  • A cost baseline tied to outcomes, not just usage.
  • A prioritized savings list, ranked by effort against expected impact.
  • A lightweight process to keep spend visible after we leave.

How we know it worked

  • Spend per unit of value — not just total spend — moves in the right direction.
  • Someone inside the company can explain this quarter's AI bill without calling us.

Pillar 04

Platform consolidation / golden-provider migration

The situation

"We're running Claude, ChatGPT, Copilot, and a couple of others across different teams with no plan. We want to standardize on one platform without breaking how people actually work."

What's really going on

Tool sprawl happened the same way the playground problem did — bottom-up adoption, no direction. Now it's a cost, security, and support burden, but a top-down mandate to "just use X" fails if it silently breaks workflows that took months to get right. The real work is migration, not decree.

What we do

  • Inventory how AI tools are actually used, team by team — the real use case, not the sanctioned one.
  • Recommend, or validate your choice of, a golden provider based on your actual workloads, not vendor marketing.
  • Do the hands-on migration work ourselves: rebuild workflows on the new platform without losing functionality or outcomes, working directly with the teams affected.

What you walk away with

  • One standardized platform, with a paper trail of what changed and why, team by team.
  • Workflows that survived the move — same or better outcomes, on the new tool.
  • A model for evaluating the next platform decision yourselves.

How we know it worked

  • Legacy platforms are decommissioned, not just deprioritized.
  • Teams report the new platform does what the old ones did — ideally with less friction, not more.

Pillar 05

AI in the SDLC

The situation

"We want AI to actually speed up how we build software — not just autocomplete in the editor, the whole cycle."

What's really going on

Most organizations' AI use clusters at the write-code step, because that's where the tools are most visible. The bigger opportunity — and the harder one — is upstream and downstream of that: requirements and definition, test generation and coverage, release and deployment, and maintenance/on-call. Left unaddressed, code-writing gets faster while everything around it stays the bottleneck.

What we do

  • Map your define → build → test → release → maintain cycle and find where AI reduces cycle time without reducing quality.
  • Introduce and integrate tools and practices at each stage to fit your existing process, not a wholesale rip-and-replace.
  • Measure cycle time and quality before and after, stage by stage.

What you walk away with

  • A faster, still-trustworthy SDLC, with AI doing real work at more than one stage.
  • Documented practices your team can extend on its own.
  • A before/after picture you can take to leadership.

How we know it worked

  • Cycle time drops at the stages we touched, with defect and incident rates flat or improved.
  • The team keeps using what we introduced after we're gone.
