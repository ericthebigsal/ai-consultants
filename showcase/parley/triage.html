Keep, harden, retire

Every feature sorted against the bar. Keep means it clears the bar or is one small fix away. Harden means the value is real but it isn't safe to depend on yet. Retire means the honest answer is that it's costing more than it returns — and hardening a negative-value feature just makes it an expensive negative-value feature.

Ticket triage

keep

Clears four of five rules. The gaps are the missing eval and the lack of a human gate on genuine P1s. It's two fixes from the bar, not a rebuild — and six months of agent-corrected labels are sitting there ready to become an eval set.

~1 week — build the eval from the corrected labels; add a rule that anything scored P1 goes to a human queue regardless of routing confidence.

Conversation summary

keep

Internal-only, the lowest blast radius of the six, already has an owner, a recent review, logging, and a set of graded examples. The only real gap is that the graded examples don't run in CI.

~2 days — move the notebook checks into the CI eval suite and write the quality bar down as a number.

Suggested replies

harden

Clears the labelling, human-gate, and ownership rules. Fails the eval rule, and logging is partial. The subtle risk: agents have come to trust it and now paste it with light edits, so a confidently wrong draft — a made-up refund window, a policy that doesn't exist — slips through under a human gate that has gone soft.

~2 weeks — an eval on factual accuracy and tone; a grounding check that flags any specific claim the sources don't support; full logging.

Answer bot

harden

The only customer-facing model in the product, and it fails four of five rules. Around one answer in twelve contains a claim its sources don't support. But the deflection is real value and customers do like a fast answer — so the call is to harden it properly, not kill it. Walked all the way through on Hardened.

~4 weeks — failure-mode catalogue, a retrofitted eval in CI, grounding + a confidence gate + a topic denylist, then a staged re-release.

Macro suggestions

retire

When it was finally measured it picks the wrong macro about 15% of the time, and it adds a click even when it's right. Usage telemetry shows agents dismiss the suggestion roughly 80% of the time — they've already learned not to trust it. Hardening it would cost weeks to reach parity with the plain macro search it replaced.

~2 days — remove the suggestion slot, restore the macro-library search that predated it.

Sentiment flagging

retire

No owner, no eval, and a back-test against manager-confirmed escalations shows it misses about 40% of genuinely at-risk conversations — the polite-but-furious ones — while flagging calm threads on strong language. That's worse than the keyword rule it replaced.

~1 day — disable the flag, restore the keyword rule, log the decision and the back-test so a future rebuild starts from it.

The two retirements

Retire doesn't mean rip out and walk away

Both features have a pre-AI fallback that still works. Retiring them is a small, reversible change — done deliberately, not left to rot.

  • fallback Each retirement restores the mechanism that existed before — macro search, the keyword escalation rule. No capability actually disappears.
  • comms A one-paragraph note to each owning team: what's being turned off, why, the back-test, and what it would take to bring it back as a real project.
  • watch Agent-satisfaction and escalation metrics watched for a month after each change, so a wrong call is caught fast.
  • door open Either could return — but as a scoped feature with an eval and an owner, going through the bar like anything new. Not as a revived experiment flag.
Why this is the hard part. The audit and the bar are mostly mechanical. The judgement is in the two retire calls — saying out loud that a shipped feature someone is proud of is costing more than it returns. That's why the verdict is the owning team's to make, with the evidence laid out, not ours to impose.
