The answer bot, from demo to dependency

One feature, taken all the way through hardening. The answer bot was a hackathon win that reached every eligible customer without ever being evaluated. This is what it took to make it safe to depend on — without losing the deflection that made it worth keeping.

~4 weeks unsupported-claim rate 8.3% → 0.6% deflection held

01 · As it shipped

A demo that quietly became infrastructure

A Growth engineer built it in a hackathon: retrieve the top few help-center articles for the customer's question, hand them to a model, post the answer straight into the ticket before an agent sees it. It shipped behind a flag, the flag went to 100% of eligible tickets in a week, and the launch dashboard showed deflection up. Nobody looked closer for nine months.

No eval. No confidence threshold — it answered every question at any retrieval quality. No topic limits, so it took billing disputes and security questions as readily as “how do I export a report.” No way to say “I don't know.” And no log of which help-center revision any given answer was based on.

What it actually did wrong

The blocker. One customer-facing answer in twelve carried a claim the sources didn't support. That set the release bar: the unsupported-claim rate had to come under 1% before the bot went back to full traffic.

04 · The guardrails

Four changes, each tied to a failure mode

  • grounding Citation required. Every factual sentence maps to a retrieved source above a relevance threshold. No supporting source, the sentence doesn't ship — kills most unsupported claims and the macro leakage.
  • confidence Confidence gate. Below a retrieval-quality threshold, the bot doesn't answer — it posts a short “a support agent will follow up” and routes the ticket, with the draft attached for the agent.
  • scope Topic denylist. Billing disputes, legal, security, and account cancellation skip the bot entirely and go straight to a human queue.
  • recency Freshness weighting. Retrieval down-ranks articles superseded by a newer one; the editor flow now marks the old one archived.
The shape of the fix. None of this is a better model or a longer prompt. It's a retrieval threshold, a routing rule, a denylist, and a recency signal — boring, testable plumbing around the model, which is where the reliability was missing.

Deflection dipped a single point — the questions the bot now declines are the ones it was getting wrong anyway. Customers who used to get a confident wrong answer now get a fast handoff, and the CSAT number moved because of it.

Re-release was staged: 10% of eligible tickets for a week with the eval running against live samples, then 50%, then full. The rollback trigger was the unsupported-claim rate crossing 1% on live traffic.

06 · What stayed a human call

The decisions the bar deliberately leaves open

  • The confidence threshold. Set jointly by Growth and Support — too high and deflection craters, too low and weak answers ship. It's a product trade-off, reviewed monthly against the eval and the deflection number.
  • The denylist topics. Which subjects a model should never handle customer-facing is a policy question, not a modelling one. Support and Legal own the list.
  • Whether it should be customer-facing at all. The standing question. For now the answer is yes, with the guardrails — revisited each quarter against real traffic and the incident log.
What Parley keeps. The eval suite and its question set, the guardrail code as a reusable pattern (see Enablement), the staged re-release runbook, and a named owner on the Growth team with a monthly review on the calendar.
